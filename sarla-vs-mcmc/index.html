Nonlinear inversion · SARLA versus MCMC

How do we explore a complex solution space?

Nonlinear inverse problems can have several equally good solutions connected by curved regions of uncertainty. Here, a three-parameter carbon-turnover problem shows how SARLA and MCMC approach that geometry—and how well each recovers the posterior distribution.

SARLA: find modes, learn geometry, then correct

  1. Start with 16 random parameter vectors. Use JAX Jacobians in bounded Gauss–Newton optimization to move toward good fits. These optimized solutions seed the atlas; they are computed as part of SARLA.
  2. At each new chart, compute the exact Hessian with JAX. Its eigenvectors orient the chart; its eigenvalues set curvature-dependent widths and identify weakly constrained directions.
  3. Build an atlas: a collection of local probability models, or “charts.” Audit and repair poorly represented regions.
  4. Freeze the atlas and use it to propose Metropolis–Hastings moves. Acceptance correction targets the desired posterior.

MCMC: build a distribution through repeated moves

The goal is a distribution of plausible parameters. A single optimized point tells us where the fit is good; a posterior also describes uncertainty, correlations, and alternative solutions.

SARLA’s final correction is itself MCMC. This comparison tests whether its learned proposal improves exploration over the selected direct sampler.

Where a Gaussian approximation stops

Why two modes?

Swapping τ₁ and τ₂ while replacing f by 1 − f leaves the prediction unchanged. The two modes include this exact label symmetry. They do not, by themselves, establish two distinct physical mechanisms.

Why can a bridge appear?

With fewer observations, many parameter combinations make similar predictions. The high-probability region broadens and can connect the two label orderings. The prior still bounds the allowed solutions.

The real problem and its assumptions
M(t) = 100 [f exp(−t/τ₁) + (1 − f) exp(−t/τ₂)]

The example fits measured organic matter remaining in a litter-decomposition experiment: Bouteloua gracilis, Cedar Creek, UV-block treatment, Brandt et al. (2010), series CBB in aridec v1.0.2. Time is in months; M is percent of initial organic matter. The four observations are (2.5, 84.21), (6, 73.09), (12, 61.08), and (24, 47.23). The two-observation setting uses the first two. Initial 100% normalization is excluded from the likelihood.

The analysis assumes independent Gaussian residuals with σ = 2 percentage points and a uniform prior in (log₁₀τ₁, log₁₀τ₂, f), with log₁₀τ in [−0.3, 2.3] and f in [0.02, 0.98]. The uncertainty scale is an analysis assumption, not the experiment’s published measurement error.

What this comparison establishes—and its limits

These are precomputed runs of the unmodified SARLA2 engine, with JAX derivatives, alongside JAX NUTS, a local random walk and emcee differential-evolution/snooker moves (80%/20%). Numerical integration on a 216³ grid supplies the reference, checked against 144³. The 18³-bin differences between those references are 0.17 percentage points with four observations and 0.10 with two. The reported comparison uses 18³ scoring bins; the earlier optimized-start analysis also examined other bin sizes.

The replay uses seed 3; the results table summarizes seeds 3, 17, and 41. The tighter audit threshold was selected after inspecting the seed-3 pilot, then checked with the additional seeds. This is exploratory tuning on one small problem. SARLA begins with 16 random points inside the prior (seed 1729), then uses JAX-based Gauss–Newton optimization to find starting solutions; the observed four-data solutions include both label orderings. The two gradient-free MCMC baselines begin directly from 64 independent uniform prior draws for each seed, with no optimization or derivatives and a NumPy likelihood. The additional JAX NUTS baseline uses 32 random-prior chains with gradients, 512 warm-up and 1,536 production steps per chain. SARLA’s original runs are retained; both direct MCMC baselines were rerun from random starts. The three SARLA sampling seeds share one mode-finding seed, so they do not measure the reliability of discovering every mode. NUTS explicitly uses JAX; only the two gradient-free baselines use NumPy.

The orange chart outlines are one-standard-deviation ellipses of SARLA’s local Gaussian proposal components, clipped to the prior box. They are not posterior credible regions. The SARLA audit replay reconstructs the original deterministic chart states and checks final centers and audit records against the saved benchmark. Sampling views use actual stored states, thinned and quantized for display; all reported scores use the full original samples.

The original methods have different warm-up and production lengths within approximately the same target-evaluation budget; the added NUTS runs use a different gradient-work budget. Stage 1 aligns SARLA optimization progress with MCMC warm-up progress; stages 2–3 show atlas repair and post-warm-up sampling. These phase replays are normalized independently and do not compare elapsed time or proposal counts. SARLA paths contain actual accepted optimizer iterates; MCMC paths contain saved chain states. Finite MCMC samples still have error; a valid stationary distribution is not evidence that a finite run has converged.
