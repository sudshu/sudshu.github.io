About this example and its parameters

Nonlinear inverse problems can have complex, multidimensional solution spaces, with multiple peaks, curved ridges, and narrow connections between plausible solutions. This page uses a two-component decay model fitted to observations of decomposing plant material to explore how different parameter combinations can explain the same data—a challenge shared by atmospheric inversions, seismology, and other inverse problems.

The unknowns being estimated are three parameters, collected in the parameter vector θ = (τ₁, τ₂, f). Each plotted point is one candidate parameter vector; “Best fit” selects an optimized estimate.

The forward problem

The forward model maps three unknown parameters, θ = (τ₁, τ₂, f), to a predicted observation M(t): the sum of two exponentially decaying components. In this application, the components represent stores of decomposing plant material, called pools in carbon-cycle science. Each loses material at a rate proportional to its remaining amount, and observations measure their combined remaining mass at specified times.

Component 2: 1 − f

dC₁/dt = −C₁/τ₁

M(t) = 100[f exp(−t/τ₁) + (1−f) exp(−t/τ₂)]

The inverse problem estimates θ from the observations yᵢ at times tᵢ, accounting for assumed errors and prior information. The posterior describes the parameter combinations supported by those choices and the data. Here, the observed quantity is the percentage of plant organic matter remaining; this example illustrates carbon-pool inference using a simplified model.

Why this solution space is interesting

Two modes can describe exactly the same decay curve. Swapping τ₁ and τ₂ while replacing f by 1−f leaves M(t) unchanged. Those two peaks represent the same model prediction under different component labels. “Swap component labels” demonstrates this with the same predicted observations.

The data also leave genuine parameter trade-offs. Different component fractions and decay times can produce similar predictions at a few observation times. Use the first two observations to expose broad curved regions, then restore all four. At the default error scale, the region where the two decay times are equal is much less plausible with all four observations. Any visible connection depends on the selected density threshold and the finite grid.

A local quadratic approximation cannot explain the whole geometry. The component-label symmetry and curved trade-offs arise from the forward equations and these observations. No extra peaks, bridges, or warping functions were added to the posterior.

Statistical assumptions, coordinates, and numerical checks

The likelihood uses independent Gaussian residuals, with the selected common σ. The prior is uniform in (log₁₀τ₁, log₁₀τ₂, f), with decay times between 10−0.3 and 102.3 months (about 0.50–200 months), and f between 0.02 and 0.98. Thus the density shown is with respect to log decay-time coordinates and the fraction, not linear decay-time coordinates. These prior bounds and the error model are analysis choices, not claims from the original study.

J(θ) = Σᵢ [M(tᵢ; θ) − yᵢ]² / (2σ²)
p(θ | y) / pmax = exp[−(J(θ) − Jmin)]
p(log τ₁, log τ₂ | y) ∝ ∫ p(log τ₁, log τ₂, f | y) df

The colors use viridis, a sequential color scale with increasing lightness, applied to log relative density. Transparency helps expose the volume interior, so overlapping dots can blend colors; use the flat slice for an unobscured view of the density.

The three-parameter cloud uses a regular 65 × 65 × 81 grid in the displayed prior coordinates. Its dots are volume-rendering elements, not Monte Carlo samples. The two-parameter surface marginalizes over f by midpoint quadrature (121 nodes); its height is relative log marginal density. Surface triangles are clipped at the selected ΔJ, and regions below the cutoff are left uncolored rather than drawn as a flat floor. The two views each have their own maximum for the color scale. Peak density and integrated probability mass are different quantities.

The cutting plane is defined in normalized display coordinates u₁ = (log₁₀τ₁ − 1)/1.3, u₂ = (log₁₀τ₂ − 1)/1.3, u₃ = (f − 0.5)/0.48. Its equation is n·u = d. When enabled, the cut hides n·u > d. The flat slice directly evaluates the full three-parameter density on that plane and uses the volume's color scale. A slice is not a marginal distribution. Turning on τ₁ ≤ τ₂ applies the same ordering mask in all views.

For the selected observations, the optimum fraction at any pair of decay times is found analytically by bounded linear least squares. A grid search followed by a pattern search locates a best fit. For all four observations, an independent numerical optimization gives approximately τfast = 3.284 months, τslow = 46.903 months, ffast = 0.2139, with squared residual sum 0.4475 percentage-points². The model and observations are embedded in this file; no server, external library, WebGL, or network connection is needed.

With only four nonzero-time observations and a simplified stationary model, this is an explainer of an actual inverse problem, not a validated ecological parameter product. It does not model seasonal forcing, microbial transfers, or every cause of litter loss. Broader discussion of identifiability in decomposition data: Sarquis & Sierra (2023).
