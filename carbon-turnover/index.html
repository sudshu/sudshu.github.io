The forward problem

Put the initial litter into two independently decomposing pools. Each loses material at a rate proportional to its remaining amount. The model predicts the total organic matter remaining at every harvest.

Pool 1: f
turnover τ₁
+
Pool 2: 1 − f
turnover τ₂
dC₁/dt = −C₁/τ₁
dC₂/dt = −C₂/τ₂
M(t) = 100[f exp(−t/τ₁) + (1−f) exp(−t/τ₂)]

The inverse problem runs this relationship backward: find which (τ₁, τ₂, f) explain the observed totals. This is a small compartment-model problem related to carbon-pool inference. Litter organic matter is the measured quantity here; it is not a direct observation of pure carbon or a CARDAMOM assimilation.

Why this solution space is interesting

Two modes can describe exactly the same decay curve. Swapping τ₁ and τ₂ while replacing f by 1−f leaves M(t) unchanged. Those two peaks are a label symmetry, not evidence for two distinct ecosystem mechanisms. “Swap pool labels” demonstrates this with the same predicted observations.

The data also leave genuine parameter trade-offs. A smaller rapidly decomposing fraction and a different slow turnover time can give similar total mass at a few harvests. Use the first two harvests to expose broad curved regions, then restore all four. At the default error scale, the equal-turnover region is much less plausible with all four harvests. Any visible connection depends on the selected density threshold and the finite grid.

A local quadratic approximation cannot explain the whole geometry. The pool symmetry and curved trade-offs arise from the forward equations and these observations. No extra peaks, bridges, or warping functions were added to the posterior.

Statistical assumptions, coordinates, and numerical checks

The likelihood uses independent Gaussian residuals, with the selected common σ. The prior is uniform in (log₁₀τ₁, log₁₀τ₂, f), with turnover times between 10−0.3 and 102.3 months (about 0.50–200 months), and f between 0.02 and 0.98. Thus the density shown is with respect to log turnover coordinates and the fraction, not linear turnover coordinates. These prior bounds and the error model are analysis choices, not claims from the original study.

J(θ) = Σᵢ [M(tᵢ; θ) − yᵢ]² / (2σ²)
p(θ | y) / pmax = exp[−(J(θ) − Jmin)]
p(log τ₁, log τ₂ | y) ∝ ∫ p(log τ₁, log τ₂, f | y) df

The three-parameter cloud uses a regular 65 × 65 × 81 grid in the displayed prior coordinates. Its dots are volume-rendering elements, not Monte Carlo samples. The two-parameter surface marginalizes over f by midpoint quadrature (121 nodes); its height is relative log marginal density, clipped at the selected ΔJ. The two views each have their own maximum for the color scale. Peak density and integrated probability mass are different quantities.

The cutting plane is defined in normalized display coordinates u₁ = (log₁₀τ₁ − 1)/1.3, u₂ = (log₁₀τ₂ − 1)/1.3, u₃ = (f − 0.5)/0.48. Its equation is n·u = d. When enabled, the cut hides n·u > d. The flat slice directly evaluates the full three-parameter density on that plane and uses the volume's color scale. A slice is not a marginal distribution. Turning on τ₁ ≤ τ₂ applies the same ordering mask in all views.

For the selected harvests, the optimum fraction at any pair of turnover times is found analytically by bounded linear least squares. A grid search followed by a pattern search locates a best fit. For all four harvests, an independent numerical optimization gives approximately τfast = 3.284 months, τslow = 46.903 months, ffast = 0.2139, with squared residual sum 0.4475 percentage-points². The model and observations are embedded in this file; no server, external library, WebGL, or network connection is needed.

With only four nonzero-time observations and a simplified stationary model, this is an explainer of an actual inverse problem, not a validated ecological parameter product. It does not model seasonal forcing, microbial transfers, or every cause of litter loss. Broader discussion of identifiability in decomposition data: Sarquis & Sierra (2023).
